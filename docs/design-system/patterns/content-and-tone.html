Content and tone

Shelfie talks to a nine-year-old and to her dad, often about the same thing. Nia hears a friendly coach who is proud of her. Dad hears a tidy assistant who tells him what is waiting.

Overview

The base rules (sentence case, the error formula, the empty-state formula, numbers and dates) live in foundations: content. This page covers what is particular to Shelfie: which voice to use where, how the same fact is said to each person, and the exact strings the mocks already use, so new copy matches them.

When to use

The voice follows the reader of the screen, not the subject of the sentence.

  • Reader shell pages (shelf, book, add a book, log a chapter, chapter answers) and the sign-in page use Nia's voice.
  • Grown-up shell pages (Review, Books, Manage book, Settings), the password sign-in and the three dialogs use Dad's voice.
  • Shared surfaces (the profile picker, error pages, the offline banner) use Nia's voice, because she is the one most likely to see them; they still read fine to Dad.
  • Words Dad writes for Nia (feedback notes, reward notes) are his own. Help text nudges him toward her voice: "She reads this on her own, so keep it short and warm."

Responsive behaviour

  • Write for a 360px phone first. A button label that wraps at 360px is too long; shorten the verb phrase.
  • Book titles are never shortened in copy. They wrap (balanced) in headings and truncate to one line only in dense rows (trophies, the review queue), with the full title in title.
  • Subtitles joined by middots ("🔥 5-day streak · 2 books on the go · 1 reward waiting") may wrap; keep each part meaningful on its own.
  • Paragraphs stop at about 65 characters wide (--layout-measure); empty-state text at 36.

Accessibility

Role and pattern

Plain language is the pattern: Nia is nine, and a reading app must not be hard to read. Aim for short words and sentences she can read herself. Every page has lang="en".

Keyboard

KeyAction
TabEvery link and button label makes sense when read alone in a list of controls ("Review Chapter 10", not "Click here").

Focus

Headings and the first sentence carry the point, so a screen reader that lands on a new page's <h1> hears what matters first.

Labelling

Emoji are decoration. When an emoji stands alone as an icon (a cover sticker, a trophy) it is aria-hidden. When it sits inside a sentence ("Hi Nia 👋") it is read, so put it at the end. Never let an emoji carry meaning alone: "⭐ Dad read it", not "⭐". Short or ambiguous text gets a fuller aria-label: the streak pill "5 days" is labelled "5-day reading streak"; the count "2" is labelled "2 chapters to review".

Motion

Words never depend on motion: a celebration's heading says the news even when confetti is turned off by reduced motion.

Touch

Labels on touch targets are a few words, so targets can stay large without wrapping: "I’m done!" fits a 52px button on any phone.

Less is more

A headline plus at most one short subtitle line. Help text only where a wrong entry is likely (the username rule, the password minimum). A field error is one short sentence. Summary items are two to four words ("Fix the e-mail"). An alert is a title plus at most one short sentence.

Case and punctuation

  • Sentence case everywhere, including buttons, titles and badges as written ("Reward to give"); badges are set in capitals by style, not by typing them.
  • Curly quotes and apostrophes: ’ “ ”. A middot with spaces ( · ) joins facts. An em dash with spaces joins a clause in celebration text only.
  • Ellipsis (…) only on progress words: "Saving…".
  • No full stop on titles, labels, buttons or single-line toast titles; full stops on sentences in body text.
